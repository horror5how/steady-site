## The rules

1. Most of the conversion gain sits above the fold, the first screen on a phone before any scroll. Spend the effort there. (A)
2. About 90% of his landing page tests are the headline or the image under it. Test those two first, one test a week. (A)
3. If a new headline makes the old image wrong, test the new headline and new image together against the old pair. (A)
4. Above the fold means headline, sub-headline and image. Those three decide whether someone stays. (A)
5. The headline is worth most of the page. A strong one can beat the control by two to five times; a normal test is a 20% lift. (B)
6. Every page has a headline, good or bad. Curiosity, something different, and a desirable result are what make it good. (B)
7. Steal headline shapes from other industries: look at the first three seconds of winning ads and organic posts elsewhere and borrow the structure. (B)
8. Headline formula: a time frame, then the result shown in a picture the reader can see, then a naming word like challenge, blueprint or programme. (D)
9. Show the result, do not name it: "drop two belt notches" beats "lose weight". Describe the moment the problem goes away. (B, D)
10. The sub-headline is the lead-in. It ties straight back to the result in the headline and makes them want the next line. (D)
11. The page must match the ad that sent them. Same promise, same words, same result. Lazy pages that change the message lose the click they paid for. (D)
12. Call out who it is for and, more strongly, who it is not for. Saying "this isn't for you if" makes the right person lean in. (B, D)
13. Rule of one: one big idea per page. It must be easy to understand, easy to believe, and interesting. Every bullet below it only supports that one idea. (E)
14. Mixing several selling points is "toss salad" copy. Of the top 100 offers his source studied, 91 centred on a single idea. (E)
15. Buyers decide on one issue. Every bullet costs reading effort, so each one has to be worth the whole decision on its own. Fewer bullets, each one perfect. (F)
16. Keep the page simple enough to decide on fast. Amazon and Airbnb convert because the page is short and clear. (F)
17. Each line sells the next line, the page headline sells the one next step. Ask for one step only. A confused mind does not click. (B)
18. Clear call to action: tell them exactly what to tap and what happens next. If you do not say it, fewer people do it. (B)
19. Give a reason why for the next step. Any honest reason beats no reason. (B)
20. Write at a third-grade reading level: short sentences, small words, big promise, big proof, better verbs instead of adverbs. (B)
21. Proof beats promise, and nobody can copy proof. Once they want it, the next question is "why should I believe this?" (B)
22. Implied authority: say what only you can truthfully say (the method, the credential, the source). Never more than is true. (B)
23. Value Equation: value goes up with the dream outcome and the believed likelihood of getting it; it goes down with the time delay and the effort and sacrifice. (C)
24. The fortunes are in the bottom half: cut the wait and cut the effort. Amazon sells the same things as everyone else and wins on one click and next-day delivery. (C)
25. The four parts of the equation are also the parts of a headline or claim: the outcome, why it will work for you, how fast, and what it takes. (C)
26. The end of the page is the second most read part after the headline (his "PS" rule). Close with a one-line recap or a "who this is not for" line, then the button. (B)
27. Spell out any promise in plain words. "Results guaranteed" is ignored; a specific, plain statement is read. (C)

## Applied to Steady

What Steady can truthfully say today: it is a free AI voice app, it uses exposure and response prevention (ERP, the practice of facing a thought without doing the checking or reassurance it demands), it is for adults in the US, and the way in is a 2-minute, 9-question application. There are no users, no testimonials and no outcome numbers yet, so none appear below. Proof has to come from the method and from showing how it works.

### The Value Equation, filled in

- Dream outcome: intrusive and looping thoughts stop running your day. You notice the thought and get on with what you were doing.
- Believed likelihood: it uses ERP, the science-backed method built for exactly this, and it refuses to reassure you, because reassurance is what keeps the loop going. Show a short real clip of a Steady conversation so they see it before they believe it.
- Time delay: a 2-minute application, then you start talking to Steady. No waiting list, no appointment.
- Effort and sacrifice: free, no card, voice only so nothing to type, use it at any time of day from your phone.

### Hero headline, 3 options

1. Don't let intrusive thoughts win.
2. Intrusive thoughts lose when you stop feeding them.
3. Stuck on the same thought again? Don't let it win today.

Option 1 is the control. Option 2 adds curiosity and hints at the mechanism (rule 6). Option 3 calls out the reader in their own moment (rule 12). Test them one a week (rule 2).

A fourth to test later, using his time frame plus result plus naming word formula (rule 8): "2 minutes to start: the Steady thought practice." Weaker on emotion, stronger on low effort.

### Sub-headline options

1. Steady is a free AI voice that helps you practise ERP, the science-backed method for intrusive and looping thoughts. It won't reassure you. It helps you stop needing to.
2. Talk it through out loud with a voice that won't feed the loop. Free, for adults in the US, and you can start today.
3. The thought shows up. You stop checking, stop asking, stop arguing with it. Steady walks you through that, out loud, whenever it hits.

### CTA button text options

1. Start my 2-minute application
2. See if Steady fits me (2 min)
3. Take the 9 questions, start today

Risk line under the button, every time: Free. No card. 2 minutes. Adults 18+ in the US. Not therapy.

### Page section order (mobile)

1. Hero: headline, sub-headline, one image of an ordinary adult mid-thought in daylight (on a train, at a desk, at the kettle; not at night), the button, the risk line. Nothing else above the fold.
2. The loop, named: three short lines showing the moment (the thought arrives, you check or ask or argue, it comes back louder). Show it, do not explain it.
3. The one big idea: reassurance feeds the loop, so Steady won't reassure you. Steady helps you face the thought and let it sit until it loses its pull. This is the rule-of-one section.
4. How it works, three steps: answer 9 questions (2 minutes), talk to Steady out loud, practise not doing the check. Button.
5. Why believe it: ERP is the science-backed method for this, plus a 30 to 45 second captioned clip of a real Steady conversation. No testimonials until real ones exist with consent.
6. Who this is for and who it is not for: for adults whose thoughts loop and pull them into checking or reassurance. Not for anyone in crisis, under 18, or outside the US, with a real link for each.
7. Two objections answered in one line each: "Is this just ChatGPT?" and "What does it cost?"
8. Close: one-line recap ("Free, 2 minutes, and you start today. Don't let the thought win."), then the button again.
