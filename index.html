Which one is your loop?

Four patterns. The same engine underneath. Every one of them is trainable — and the science backs it.

Looping thoughts

“The same thought has been playing all day. Arguing with it just makes it louder.”

A worry, a conversation, a what-if — on repeat like a song you can't turn off.

What actually helps

Pushing a thought away makes it bounce back stronger — psychologists proved it in 1987 (the “white bear” effect). Saying it out loud and mapping it does the opposite.

Wegner et al., 1987 →

The reassurance-seeking mind

“Just tell me it's okay. Okay but… are you sure?”

You ask. You feel better for ten minutes. Then the doubt comes back, hungrier. Partners, friends, Google — never quite enough.

What actually helps

Research calls reassurance a “safety behavior”: relief now, more anxiety later, because your brain never gets to learn it was fine. Gently practicing not-asking is the exit.

Salkovskis model · review, 2020 →

The compulsion

“One more check, and then I'll be able to relax.”

Check the lock. Reread the email. Count it, redo it, get it “just right”. The action buys quiet — and quietly teaches your brain the fear was real.

What actually helps

Practicing the trigger without the ritual (called ERP) is the most-proven approach there is — large, lasting improvements across 37 clinical trials.

Öst et al., meta-analysis, 2015 →

Anxiety that won't leave

“It follows me — dinner, the shower, 2am. I just want my head back.”

Not real danger. Just a thought that clamps on and drags anxiety behind it, everywhere you go.

What actually helps

94% of people get intrusive thoughts — a 13-country study proved it. The difference isn't the thought. It's how you respond to it. And that part is trainable.

Radomsky et al., 6 continents, 2014 →

All four run on the same engine. The same gentle practice unwinds them.
