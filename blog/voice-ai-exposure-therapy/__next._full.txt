<p>Every mental-health app faces the same question: where does the problem actually live? For rumination and compulsive loops, the answer is precise. It lives in language, in your inner voice, usually at speed, usually at night.</p>
<h2>The typing problem</h2>
<p>A worksheet asks you to summarise your fear in a text box. But the loop is not a summary. It is a live, fast, spoken argument in your head. By the time you have typed it, you are describing the storm from the shore. Useful, but not training.</p>
<h2>What speaking changes</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Same channel as the loop.</strong> Saying the fear out loud engages the verbal machinery the rumination runs on. You practise where the problem lives.</li>
<li><strong>No hiding.</strong> Your voice carries the tremor a text box hides. A voice partner can meet you at the actual intensity, not the typed version of it.</li>
<li><strong>Hands and eyes free.</strong> Exposure often happens in the real world: at the door you want to re-check, in the kitchen, on a walk. Voice goes where the trigger is.</li>
<li><strong>Presence.</strong> At the peak of a wave, a calm voice saying "stay here, this is the practice working" does something a progress bar never will.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Back and forth matters more than voice alone</h2>
<p>Voice notes and guided meditations are one-directional. The therapeutic moment is responsive: you say the urge is spiking, and what comes back meets that moment. Steady runs on real-time speech-to-speech AI, so the conversation is a genuine exchange, with the latency of a person, not a phone menu.</p>
<h2>And the one thing the voice will not say</h2>
<p>Steady's voice is warm, but it is built on a hard rule: it never gives reassurance, because reassurance is the compulsion. A voice that comforts you by answering the doubt would be the most pleasant possible way to keep you stuck. The kindness is in the refusal.</p>
