<p>Rumination is the replay. The conversation from Tuesday examined again. The health worry researched one more time. The "what kind of person does that make me" audit at 2am. It feels like thinking. It is not.</p>
<h2>Thinking solves. Rumination circles.</h2>
<p>Real problem-solving has an exit: you reach a decision and stop. Rumination has no exit because it is not trying to reach one. Its actual job is to make uncertainty feel temporarily managed. It is a <strong>mental compulsion</strong>, the invisible cousin of checking a lock. Nobody sees you do it, which is why people can ruminate for years without anyone, including themselves, calling it what it is.</p>
<h2>Three signs it's rumination, not thought</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>You have been here before.</strong> Same question, same evidence, tenth visit.</li>
<li><strong>Certainty is the goal.</strong> You are not looking for an answer. You are looking for a feeling of "settled" that never lasts.</li>
<li><strong>It ends by exhaustion, not conclusion.</strong> You stop because you are tired, not because you finished.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Why you can't out-think it</h2>
<p>The loop runs on your reasoning engine. Using more reasoning to fight it is giving it more fuel. The way out is not a better argument. It is practising a different response: noticing the pull, naming it, and letting the question sit unanswered while you return to your actual life. Uncomfortable, repeatable, and the only thing that reliably works.</p>
<h2>Practising the exit</h2>
<p>This skill is trainable, like a muscle. In Steady, you map your specific loops out loud, then run short guided practices where the pull shows up and you do not follow it, with a voice coaching you through the discomfort. The rep is small. The reps add up. People do not stop ruminating by winning the argument. They stop by proving, dozens of times, that the argument does not need them to attend.</p>
